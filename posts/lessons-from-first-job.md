# 5 Things Nobody Told Me About My First Real Job

I graduated Cum Laude. I could build iOS apps, design REST APIs, and explain the difference between MVVM and MVC without breaking a sweat.

I thought I was ready.

I was not ready.

Here are the five things I wish someone had told me before I walked into my first professional engineering role — things that took me months of stumbling to figure out on my own.

---

## 1. Communication Is The Skill

In university, the deliverable is code. In a real company, the deliverable is *outcomes* — and outcomes require alignment.

Your stakeholders don't care about your elegant architecture. They care about whether the feature works, ships on time, and solves their problem. The engineer who ships a good-enough solution with clear communication will always outperform the one who builds something beautiful in silence.

I learned this at FPT Software, where my biggest value wasn't my code — it was translating client requirements into technical blueprints that both the business and the engineers could agree on. A 95% UAT acceptance rate on first cycle didn't come from brilliant code. It came from asking the right questions upfront.

**Lesson:** Communicate early, communicate often. When in doubt, over-communicate.

---

## 2. The Codebase Is Alive — Respect It

Walking into an existing codebase is like walking into someone else's home. There are rules you don't see on the walls — naming conventions, architectural patterns, unwritten agreements between engineers.

My first week at Dinotis, I rewrote a component the "right" way without asking anyone. My senior engineer looked at the PR and said five words I'll never forget: *"Why did you change this?"*

I had a great technical reason. But I had no idea that component was tied to three other features under active development, and my change would break two of them.

**Lesson:** Read before you write. Understand before you change. Ask before you refactor.

---

## 3. Deadlines Are a Feature, Not a Constraint

I used to think deadlines were the enemy of quality. Now I know they're the forcing function that creates it.

At FPT, we had a Distributor Management System to ship to 38 distributors across Indonesia. The deadline was fixed — the distributors had committed to a launch date, contracts were signed. We shipped on time, with 100% feature delivery.

Was it perfect? No. Were there edge cases we wished we'd handled better? Absolutely. But shipping a working system that people could actually use was infinitely more valuable than spending another month polishing something nobody had yet.

**Lesson:** Done beats perfect. Version 1 always beats version infinity.

---

## 4. The Best Engineers Are the Best Learners

When I joined my internship at Nusantara Beta Studio, I'd never written a line of UIKit code. My entire iOS experience was SwiftUI.

I had two options: tell my manager I wasn't comfortable with UIKit, or figure it out.

I stayed up for three nights reading Apple's documentation, watching WWDC sessions, and reading my colleagues' code. By the end of week two, I was contributing meaningfully to the MRT Jakarta app revamp.

Nobody in the professional world expects you to know everything. They do expect you to be relentless about closing your knowledge gaps.

**Lesson:** Your current skills got you the job. Your learning velocity will determine how far you go.

---

## 5. Your Career Is a Product — Design It

This one took the longest to sink in.

In the first year of your career, it's easy to just accept whatever comes — the projects you're assigned, the skills you happen to pick up, the team you land on. But your career doesn't build itself. You have to intentionally design it.

What kind of engineer do you want to be in 5 years? What problems do you want to solve? Who do you want to learn from?

I started asking these questions too late. But once I did, my decisions got sharper — which projects to prioritize, which opportunities to pursue, which skills to invest in.

**Lesson:** Be intentional. Your career is the most important product you'll ever ship.

---

## A Note on Imposter Syndrome

You will feel like you don't belong. Everyone does.

The engineer who seems unshakably confident in the team meeting? They went home and Googled something basic afterward. I promise.

The difference between engineers who grow fast and those who stagnate isn't talent — it's the willingness to feel uncomfortable and do the work anyway.

Show up. Ask questions. Ship things. Learn from the failures. Repeat.

That's all there is to it.

---

*Written from Jakarta, February 2024 — six months into my first full-time role. Sharing in the hope that someone a few steps behind me finds it useful.*
